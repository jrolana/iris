\chapter{Review of Related Literature}

The rapid advancement of global research and innovation has spurred the growth of Intellectual Property (IP) and Intellectual Property Rights (IPR). With this, there has been an avenue for research towards Intellectual Property Management Systems (IPMS) in businesses, universities, and research institutions. This literature review builds the context for the current state of IP and IPR in the Philippines, and further delves into the discussion of utilizing digital technologies for IP management, starting from a global perspective, narrowing down to the university level. This review aims to identify and acquire an understanding of related concepts, existing solutions, research gaps, as well as frameworks that would be essential for the development of an Intellectual Property Management System (IPMS) tailored to the Technology Transfer and Business Development Office (TTBDO) institution of the University of the Philippines Visayas (UPV).

\section{IP Management and Technology Transfer}
The Philippines recognizes the vital role of IPs in creativity, innovation, and the overall development of the nation, economically and socially; it recognizes the social function that IPs possess. In 1997, the State enacted the Republic Act No. 8923, otherwise known as the “Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines,”  which holds the power and authority to enforce all laws related to intellectual property protection. With it, the Intellectual Property Office of the Philippines (IPOPHL) was established as the national government agency responsible for administering and implementing the laws. It also serves as the central authority for the protection, regulation, and promotion of IPs.

Although the establishment of IPOPHL laid the groundwork for the system of IP protection, it lacks the function to extract commercial value from IPs. Bader et al. (2012, as cited in Agostini et al., 2017) stated that ``The mere accumulation of IP assets does not grant any value appropriation from innovation or any return on investment; to this purpose, IP management not only becomes a critical challenge for firms, but also a practice they cannot disregard if they want to reap the benefits of their IP.'' In fact, institutions that generate and protect intellectual property struggle with capitalization and extracting commercial value from them (Agostini et al., 2016, as cited in Agostini et al., 2017).  Because of this challenge in the IP field for institutions and businesses, the IP protection perspective of their research departments changed from economic and legal to strategic and managerial, as strategic IP-driven decisions have a substantial impact on institutions and businesses (Di Minin and Faems, 2013, as cited in Agostini et al., 2017).

Recognizing this need, the Philippine government enacted the Republic Act No. 10055, otherwise known as the Philippine Technology Transfer Act of 2009, with an objective that “aims to promote and facilitate the transfer, dissemination, and effective use, management, and commercialization of intellectual property, technology and knowledge resulting from R\&D funded by the government for the benefit of national economy and taxpayers.” (Republic Act No. 10055, 2009, as stated in Department of Science and Technology [DOST], 2009). The act provides a framework for the ownership, management, and commercialization of IPs that are generated from government-funded research and development. This act also mandated all Research and Development Institutions (RDI) of the country to establish their own Technology Licensing Offices (TLO) and/or Technology Business Development Offices (TBDO) that can have their own governing policies, in support of the laws and policies of the IPOPHL and the nation, regarding Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) management and Technology Transfer (Republic Act No. 10055, 2009).

In accordance with the Philippine Technology Transfer Act of 2009 (R.A. 10055), the University of the Philippines established the UP Technology Licensing Office, which was subsequently renamed the Technology Transfer and Business Development Office (TTBDO) in 2011. It is the university’s primary institution and mechanism for managing, protecting, and commercializing IPs generated from academic research, innovation, and creative works. Its primary functions include overseeing invention disclosures, facilitating intellectual property registrations and license agreements, collaborating with legal offices to enforce intellectual property rights, aiding in research collaborations, and advising researchers on commercialization plans (Technology Transfer and Business Development Office [TTBDO], n.d.).

\section{Studies on the Usability of Digital Systems in IP Management}
In this modern age, a thriving industry in research and innovation has emerged, resulting in the rapid generation of intellectual property (IP) and intellectual property rights (IPR).  Firms recognize the importance of IP and IPR by using IP-driven corporate strategies. Hence, there is a need to study the practice and research of IP management (Holgersson \& Santen, 2018). According to Kolodyazhnaya et al. (2020), the digitalization of the economic system environment has significantly altered the processes of IP management, protection, and commercialization. The paradigm shift of IP management has introduced new mechanisms of collaboration and technology transfer among universities, businesses, firms, and other organizations, emphasizing the need to adopt a system-based approach in IP management. Their study stated that, in terms of IP and IPR, the competitiveness of an institution in a socio-economic environment heavily depends on its ability to adapt to the evolving technological and digital environment. This integration of digital technologies into IP management has led to the study and the specialization of IP management systems (IPMS).

Gargate \& Momoya (2018) have explored the methods and designs of developing a structured model for an IPMS, arguing that systematic management bridges the gap between IP generation and commercialization. Jain and Sharma (2006) extend that notion with their study, an organizational perspective on IPMS. They stated that an IPMS is a mechanism that funnels IPs into strategic, revenue-generating assets that have social and economic value. Their study argues that an IPMS has a policy and managerial role, and has identified the five major functions as such:
\begin{itemize}
   \item Generation of IP
   \item IP portfolio management
   \item IP valuation
   \item Competitive assessment
   \item Strategic decision-making
\end{itemize}

Moreover, they conducted a case study of an IPMS integrated into the Institute of Indian Technology, Bombay (IITB). It has been found that the IPMS significantly contributed to the institute by increasing the rate of IP applications and technology transfers, which encourages the academe to explore new research programs.

Krejcar et al. (2020) further discuss the importance and utilization of IPMS through their study on software solutions (SWS) for IP management by university Technology Transfer Offices (TTOs). The study involved a systematic literature review of 11 identified research articles related to SWS for IP management, interviews with the TTO heads of 4 different universities, and finally an evaluation of 10 existing commercially available SWS for IP management. It has been found that technology transfer units of business institutions have different mechanisms from TTOs of universities; as such, commercial SWS are not optimal options for IP management for university TTOs. Commercial IPMSs are cost-ineffective and too complex for university-level operations; thus, the recommended IPMS for a university TTO would follow the institution’s workflow, be cost-efficient, and be customized for further specific university needs.

\subsection{Implementation Tools and Current Practices}

Through interviews with TTBDO staff, specifically Ms. Ferelyn F. Oliva (Intellectual Property Officer), the actual implementation of the IP management process reveals specifics not contained within the Citizen Charter. This involves reliance on various digital tools and manual procedures, which present challenges to the system.

\subsubsection{Application Submission}
The TTBDO solicits disclosure through text messages and email announcements given to the mother units (colleges or departments), with the expectation that these units will subsequently communicate the information to their faculty and staff constituents. This indirect communication relies on intermediary units to reach potential inventors, which results in low visibility on the system.

Once inventors have applied for IP, the exchange of IP-related documents between TTBDO and applicants is through Google Docs. While this allows for collaborative editing and reviewing of documents, it lacks formal workflow management. It is also an inefficient process since the two parties still need to manually communicate with each other, such as when a document is completed and ready for review.

\subsubsection{Tracking and Monitoring}
Application status tracking is managed manually through Google Sheets, where staff input updates at various stages of the process. Ms. Oliva noted that this manual data entry approach is vulnerable to input errors, which could result in data loss or inaccurate records, particularly since multiple users can access the same spreadsheet. While Ms. Oliva is the only one to maintain the Google Sheets, some users can access the same spreadsheets since the email that is used is handled by multiple users.

Payments are also tracked in the system. Handling payments involves technology generators, TTBDO, and IPOPHL. TTBDO receives a statement of account from IPOPHL and then informs the Technology Generator of payment requirements. Either the technology generator or the TTBDO processes payment. Once payment is received by IPOPHL, IPOPHL will then send a receipt to TTBDO. This process requires careful coordination of these parties, yet lacks a centralized system to monitor payment status. This is one of the pain points identified by Ms. Oliva, wherein the lack of payment status causes potential delays or lost documentation.

\subsubsection{Dashboard and Portfolio Monitoring}
Portfolio monitoring and dashboard are also conducted through Google Sheets, where staff use formulas for data visualizations. Like in the problem with regards to tracking, Ms. Oliva emphasized that the dependency on sensitive cell formulas and manual inputs introduces risks of accidental data corruption or loss.

\subsubsection{Data Storage Infrastracture}
The current IP management system employs a fragmented approach for storage. Staff stores data in local machines, which are applications that are not yet processed, while those that are processed are stored in Google Sheets and Google Drive. Google Drive also serves as document storage and sharing for various IP-related documents. Ms. Oliva explained that due to the distributed nature of this storage infrastructure, she has adopted practices to maintain data integrity. This includes regularly downloading local backup copies of the entire repository.

\subsubsection{Alert and Notification System}
Ms. Oliva emphasized the complete absence of automated alerts or notification mechanisms as a critical gap in their current system. IPOPHL enforces strict two-month deadlines for responding to certain application stages. Without automated reminders or deadline tracking, staff must manually monitor calendars, set personal reminders, and cascade this information to the applicants if needed. This creates significant risks of missing critical deadlines, which could result in dropping of IP applications.

\subsubsection{Processing Time}
While the Citizen Charter specifies a standard timeline of 19 days and 1 hour, Ms. Oliva noted that the actual processing time could be longer in real life. The primary factor of such delay is the concentration of responsibilities on a single IP officer.

\section{Comparative Analysis of IP Management Systems in other Universities}
Several universities, both local and international, have implemented varying approaches to IP management. UP constituents such as Diliman and Mindanao have adopted a partially digital system through the use of websites and Google Forms, respectively, to collect Invention Disclosure Forms. While the availability of downloadable forms offers greater accessibility, the overall workflow still involves manual submission and review (UP Diliman, 2020, p. 1785). 

Other local universities, such as De La Salle University (DLSU) and Polytechnic University of the Philippines (PUP), continue to rely on manual procedures. DLSU requires physical submission of IP disclosure forms (De La Salle University, n.d.), while PUP only gives out a Google Drive link to access forms, suggesting the need to submit and process applications physically. While an in-person process ensures accountability and document integrity, it might introduce challenges, including limited accessibility, increased processing time, and redundancy in document storage.

In contrast, international universities such as Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), Stanford, and Oxford University employ a fully digitalized system. The full lifecycle of an IP application is supported through an online portal, from disclosure submission to registration  (MIT TLO, 2024; OUI, 2013, p. 11). Their systems include features such as online submission portals, status tracking, and structured review workflows. 

For instance, the MIT Technology Licensing Office (TLO) outlines a digital process for submitting invention disclosures, tracking patent evaluations, and coordinating with inventors and licensees through a centralized management system (MIT TLO, Inventors’ Guide, 2021, pp. 4–7; Patenting and Commercializing Your Invention, 2020). Similarly, Stanford University’s Office of Technology Licensing describes an online disclosure submission and review workflow that routes cases through assigned licensing associates, ensuring traceable evaluation and communication (Stanford OTL, OTL’s Process, 2021). The University of Oxford’s Innovation Office also details an integrated digital platform with defined user roles, patent status monitoring, and reporting dashboards for IP portfolio management (Oxford Innovation, IP, Patents, and Licences Researcher Booklet, 2013, p. 11).

\section{Proposed Digital IP Management System}

This study proposes a web-based IP management system designed to directly address the documented gaps and inefficiencies in the current IP management process at TTBDO and other local universities, and adopt the best practices of international universities. The proposed system aims to transform TTBDO’s IP management operations by providing:
\begin{enumerate}
   \item Centralized Data Management and Storage: A unified database system to consolidate currently fragmented data across local drives, Google Sheets, and Google Drive. This ensures data integrity and eliminates the need for manual backup procedures.
   \item Automated Workflow and Tracking: Digital workflows that guide applications through each stage. The system also includes automated status tracking, deadline alerts, and notification systems to prevent missed IPOPHL deadlines and ensure timely follow-ups.
   \item Integrated Submission and Review Platform: A digital interface for technology generators to find and submit disclosures and for TTBDO staff to manage documents. This replaces the reliance on email cascading from mother units to constituents and collaboration through Google Docs.
   \item Dashboard and Reporting: A dashboard that provides visibility into application filling statistics and performance metrics of the IP output of the campus.
   \item Role-based Access and Security: Secure authentication with appropriate permissions for different users (TTBDO staff, technology generators, UP officials, and guests). This ensures proper governance of sensitive intellectual property information.

\end{enumerate}

By implementing these core requirements, the proposed system addresses the data integrity and process inefficiency concerns identified in the current manual system. The detailed functional requirements, system architecture, and technical specifications are presented in Chapter 3.

\section[Rationale for Implementing Digital Solutions in IP Management]{Rationale for Implementing Digital Solutions in IP Management}

\subsection{Current System’s Challenges}

The concentration of responsibilities on a single IP officer limits operational capacity. IP office managers need to carefully study the landscape of emerging technologies and understand how these new technologies affect them as service providers, developing strategies to absorb and take advantage of new digital technologies, and to adapt operations to fully online services (WIPO IPOBSD, n.d.). Digitalization provides productivity improvements, cost reductions, and innovations (Kraus et al., 2021), while digital systems provide the scalability necessary to handle increased IP activity and adapt to evolving technological landscapes.

Building on this concern, the current system’s reliance on manual data entry through Google Sheets and fragmented storage across local machines and Google Drive creates significant vulnerabilities. Digital transformation enables organizations to move from manual or procedural business processes to fully online, responsive and modern processes that enhance productivity and service delivery (WIPO IPOBSD, n.d.). Centralized digital systems eliminate the risks of data corruption from sensitive cell formulas and multi-user access issues, while providing systematic data protection without needing to create individual backup practices.

In addition to data management issues, the current IPs’ lack of automated alerts for IPOPHL's strict two-month deadlines represents a critical operational risk. Automated notification systems are needed to ensure tracking of deadlines and informing applicants regarding any changes to their applications. This, in turn, mitigates the risk of dropped applications due to missed deadlines.

Furthermore, challenges also exist in the area of financial coordination. The multi-party payment coordination between Technology Generators, TTBDO, and IPOPHL currently lacks centralization. Efficient integration can only be achieved through digital processes and collaborative tools (White, 2012, as cited in Kraus et al., 2021). A centralized payment monitoring system provides real-time visibility across all parties, reducing delays and preventing documentation loss that currently plagues the payment workflow.

The combination of these issues highlights the need for a holistic transformation that addresses operational bottlenecks and ensures system reliability and transparency across all IP processes.

\subsection{Benchmarking Digital IP Practices}
While local universities maintain partially digital or manual systems, international institutions demonstrate the full potential of digital IP management. The comparative analysis revealed that MIT, Stanford, and Oxford utilize integrated platforms supporting the complete lifecycle of IP applications. Digital technologies not only impact the transformation of products, business processes, or sales, but entire business models as well (Hess et al., 2016, as cited in Kraus et al., 2021). Thus, implementing a comprehensive digital solution positions UPV alongside these leading institutions.

Beyond institutional benchmarking, broader economic implications further strengthen the rationale for digital transformation. Digital Intellectual Property (IP) Rights listed robust protection of IP as one of the fundamental factors that contribute to a country's attractiveness from an investment perspective (DCO, 2024). Enhanced digital IP management capabilities strengthen institutional competitiveness and attractiveness for research collaboration and investment partnerships.

By aligning local systems with international standards, UPV not only modernizes its IP management process but also strengthens its role as a driver of innovation within the national research ecosystem.

\subsection{Strategic IP Management}
Having a dashboard to replace the manual visualizations produced by formulas in Google Sheets enables not just a truthful data representation but also offers more ways to do data visualizations and thus data analytics. WIPO IPOBSD (n.d.) states that data analytics may be used to understand trends in the IP system, moving beyond traditional statistics to deeper analysis techniques that may be used to understand micro trends, such as the distribution of filings from SMEs, or evolving technologies that may become significant in the future.

This information can be used to improve services to users, enabling IP offices to better understand the behavior of diverse users and develop strategies to better support them. Also, moving away from manual data entry and monitoring tasks enables staff to focus on strategic portfolio analysis and improved service delivery.

By equipping IP offices with tools for insight generation and trend monitoring, digital systems transform management from administrative to strategic, contributing to long-term organizational learning and decision-making.

\subsection{Institutional Impact}
The lack of a digital solution has created significant barriers, increasing the time and effort required to navigate the system, further discouraging researchers from pursuing intellectual property protection for their innovation. This cumbersome and inefficient process not only hinders innovation but also discourages collaboration and progress in the fields of research and development (Man et al., 2025).

In this context, digital technologies serve as a new channel for information or interaction with customers and other stakeholders (Li, 2020, as cited in Kraus et al., 2021). A user-friendly digital platform reduces these barriers, promoting increased participation in IP protection and supporting research commercialization efforts.

Moreover, IT facilitates information sharing and coordination, enabling the creation of value networks (Katsamakas, 2014, as cited in Kraus et al., 2021). The reshaping of customer value propositions can be achieved by using information and data analytics to enhance, extend, or redefine the customer experience (Berman, 2012, as cited in Kraus et al., 2021).

As a result, automated workflows ensure adherence to the Citizen Charter's 19-day timeline, while integrated tracking and reporting capabilities enable performance measurement and continuous improvement. If the transformation is managed effectively, IP offices will be key players in the next phase of economic development (WIPO IPOBSD, n.d.), serving both as enablers of value creation through IP and as exemplars of digital transformation for institutional services.

\section{Summary}
This section highlights the growing importance of IP management in universities, particularly under the mandates of the Philippine Technology Transfer Act of 2009. While the UPV TTBDO performs essential roles in IP protection and commercialization, its processes remain largely manual. TTBDO relies on fragmented tools such as Google Sheets, Drive, and email communication. Studies show that transforming these manual processes to digital systems enhances efficiency, data integrity, and collaboration in IP management, as evidenced by the fully digital systems of leading international institutions like MIT, Stanford, and Oxford. Thus, a web-based IPMS tailored to UPV’s TTBDO is proposed to streamline workflows, consolidate data management, enable strategic analytics, and align with international best practices. IPMS ultimately transforms the office from an administrative unit into a strategic driver of innovation and technology transfer.
